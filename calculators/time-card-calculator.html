    A weekly timesheet with unpaid lunch breaks

    Twenty-one fields — an in time, an out time and an unpaid break for each of seven days. Each row is worked out on its own, so a Tuesday with a 45-minute lunch and a Thursday with none both come out right, and the week is the sum of the seven. Every total is shown in decimal hours, because that is the form payroll wants and the form that multiplies cleanly by a rate.

    Leave a day blank and it counts as zero rather than as an error. Days off are the normal case, not an exception.

    Night shifts and shifts that cross midnight

    A row whose out time is earlier on the clock than its in time is read as running overnight, so a 10:00 pm start and a 6:30 am finish is eight and a half hours rather than a negative number. The shift is credited to the day it started on, which is the convention most timesheets use — if your employer splits an overnight shift across two days, enter it as two rows.

    Your week is saved in this browser

    Everything you type is written to this browser's local storage and read back when you return. There is no account, no upload and no server — clearing your browser data clears the timesheet, and it will not appear on another device.

    The honest value of that is not re-typing twenty-one fields. Nobody is going to abandon their payroll system for a web page, and this is not trying to be one: it is for the person who wants to check what the week actually adds up to before it goes in, and who would rather not start from an empty grid every Friday.

    How overtime is split

    By default the week is measured against the federal rule: everything past 40 hours in the workweek is overtime at one and a half times the rate (29 U.S.C. §207(a)(1)). There is no federal daily overtime rule, so under this setting four ten-hour days is forty hours and no overtime at all.

    Switch to the daily rule and each day is measured first — over 8 hours at time and a half, over 12 at double time, the pattern California uses under Labor Code §510(a) — and then, crucially, those premium hours are taken out of the week before the 40-hour test runs. California’s Court of Appeal stated the principle in Monzon v. Schaefer Ambulance Service, Inc. (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 16, 38: the weekly trigger “is reached after the employee has worked 40 hours at straight or regular time, not the first 40 hours”. A ten-hour Monday in a 42-hour week therefore yields two overtime hours, not four. Leaving them in would pay the same two hours a premium twice, which is the single most common error in timesheet arithmetic. The overtime pay calculator shows that split day by day if you want to see the working.

    Rounding is not applied anywhere. Quarter-hour and tenth-of-an-hour rounding are employer policies rather than arithmetic, they vary, and applying one here would put this page quietly out of step with your own timesheet.

    This is a wage arithmetic tool, not payroll software and not legal advice. Overtime rules vary by state and by industry — check your state labor department before relying on a figure.
