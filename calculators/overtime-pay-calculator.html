    How overtime is calculated under the federal rule

    The Fair Labor Standards Act sets one overtime rule and it is weekly: a covered, non-exempt employee must be paid at least one and a half times their regular rate for every hour worked beyond 40 in a workweek (29 U.S.C. §207(a)(1); 29 C.F.R. §778.102). There is no federal rule about long days. As a California appellate court put it while summarising the federal position, “hours worked in one day, regardless of the number, do not require overtime pay” (Huntington Memorial Hospital v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 893, fn. 1). Under federal law alone, four ten-hour days is forty hours and carries no overtime at all, and a fourteen-hour Tuesday in a thirty-hour week carries none either.

    A workweek is a fixed, recurring period of seven consecutive 24-hour days. It does not have to start on Monday, but once an employer sets it, it cannot be shifted around to dodge overtime, and hours cannot be averaged across two of them.

    Weekly overtime hours = max(0, hours worked − 40) Gross pay = (regular hours × rate) + (overtime hours × rate × 1.5)

    Daily overtime replaces the weekly calculation — it does not stack on top of it

    Several states add a daily rule on top of the federal weekly one. California's is the one most people mean: over 8 hours in a workday at time and a half, and over 12 hours in a workday at double time (Cal. Labor Code §510(a)).

    This is where most overtime calculators go wrong. When both rules are in play, an hour that has already been paid an overtime premium under the daily rule is not counted again toward the weekly 40. California’s Court of Appeal put the rule plainly in Monzon v. Schaefer Ambulance Service, Inc. (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 16: “The weekly overtime trigger of 40 hours is reached after the employee has worked 40 hours at straight or regular time, not the first 40 hours” (at p. 38). The court named the vice directly — otherwise “the daily overtime hours are effectively counted twice—once for purpose of daily overtime and again for purpose of weekly overtime” (at p. 32). The federal scheme reaches the same place from the other direction: premium pay already made at time and a half or better is credited against the overtime owed rather than added on top of it (29 U.S.C. §207(h)(2); 29 C.F.R. §778.202).

    Take a 10-hour Monday followed by four 8-hour days: 42 hours in total, with 2 of Monday's hours already paid at time and a half. If you then also treat the week as “42 hours, so 2 hours of weekly overtime”, you have paid the same two hours a premium twice, and the answer is too high.

    The correct arithmetic subtracts the daily premium hours from the pool before the weekly threshold is applied:

    Straight-time pool = total hours − daily OT hours − daily DT hours Weekly OT hours = max(0, straight-time pool − 40) Regular hours = straight-time pool − weekly OT hours

    Run the 10-hour-Monday week through that and you get 40 regular hours and 2 overtime hours — not 4. Switch the toggle above between the two rules and watch the total: on the federal rule that same week pays 40 regular and 2 overtime as well, because 42 hours minus 40 is 2. The two rules agree here by coincidence. They stop agreeing the moment the week is longer: six 10-hour days is 60 hours, which the federal rule pays as 40 regular and 20 overtime, and the daily rule pays as 40 regular and 20 overtime by a completely different route — 12 hours of daily overtime plus 8 hours of weekly overtime. Add a fourteen-hour day and only the daily rule produces any double time at all.

    Time and a half, double time, and the regular rate

    "Time and a half" is 1.5 × the regular rate, so $22 an hour becomes $33 for overtime hours. Double time is 2 ×, or $44. Both multipliers are editable above, because a union agreement or an employment contract can set a higher premium than the legal floor — the law sets a minimum, not a maximum.

    One thing this calculator cannot do for you: work out your regular rate. Under the FLSA the regular rate is not simply your base hourly wage. Non-discretionary bonuses, shift differentials and commissions have to be folded in and the rate recomputed for that workweek, which can raise the overtime rate above 1.5 × your posted hourly wage. If your pay includes any of those, the figure to type into the rate field is the recomputed regular rate, not the number on your offer letter.
